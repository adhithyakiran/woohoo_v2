14. Wake word

The wake word is the only always-on listening component, which makes it simultaneously the most privacy-sensitive and the most performance-critical piece of the device software. It must run entirely locally, with no audio leaving the device until it fires.

Decision D6 — Wake word

Keep Sensory TrulyHandsfree, upgrade the SDK, and place it behind a WakeWordEngine interface on the device (start(), onDetected(confidence, timestampMs), setSensitivity(), pause()). Qualify Picovoice Porcupine as a second implementation within six months — not to switch, but so that per-chain custom wake words become a commercial option rather than a vendor negotiation.

Required behaviours regardless of engine: a 500 ms circular pre-roll buffer so the first word after the wake phrase is never clipped; per-property sensitivity tuning (a suite by a lift shaft needs different settings than a quiet corner room); a false-accept counter reported as telemetry (a device with a high FA rate is usually next to a TV); and hard discard of the ring buffer whenever the wake word does not fire.

15. The STT placement decision: device vs cloud

This is the question you specifically asked, and it deserves a real answer rather than “it depends”. The answer is that the question is wrong as posed — speech-to-text is not one function, it is five, and they belong in different places.

15.1 Decomposing “speech to text”

Sub-functionPlacementWhy
Acoustic echo cancellationDeviceMust run before the signal leaves the mic path, or barge-in is impossible. The device knows what it is playing; the cloud does not.
Beamforming / noise suppressionDeviceRequires the raw multi-mic array. Sending N channels to the cloud multiplies bandwidth for no benefit.
Wake wordDevicePrivacy. Non-negotiable (§14).
Voice activity detection + endpointingDeviceSaves 200–400 ms per turn versus server-side endpointing, because the device can signal end-of-turn the instant it detects it rather than the cloud waiting out a silence timer over a jittery link. This is the largest single latency win available and it is free.
Transcription itselfCloudThe contested one. Analysed below.

15.2 The case for on-device transcription, taken seriously

It is a real case and it is getting stronger. Whisper-small, Moonshine and NVIDIA Parakeet-class models now run in real time on modest ARM SoCs. On-device transcription would mean: no audio ever leaves the room (which dissolves most of Part XI's consent problem), no per-minute ASR cost at all, and functioning voice control during a WAN outage. At 100,000 devices, ASR is a meaningful line item; making it zero is attractive.

Decision D1 — STT placement

Cloud streaming ASR is the primary path. The device performs AEC, beamforming, wake-word detection, VAD and endpointing, and streams Opus only between wake-word fire and end-of-turn. Audio is held in memory in the cloud for the duration of recognition and is never written to disk.

Plus a small on-device command grammar (§15.5) covering roughly 25 utterances, used only when the WAN is unavailable. This buys the outage resilience that motivates on-device ASR without paying its accuracy, hardware or maintenance costs.

Revisit trigger: re-evaluate full on-device ASR when (a) a chain contractually requires audio never to leave the property, or (b) monthly ASR spend exceeds the amortised BOM delta of a better SoC across the fleet — whichever comes first. Keep the AsrProvider interface clean so that day is a swap, not a rewrite.

15.4 The hybrid variant worth keeping in your pocket

There is a third placement nobody asks about and it fits this product unusually well: ASR on the property's Edge Gateway. Audio leaves the room but never leaves the building. A single edge appliance with a modest GPU or a capable CPU can serve a few hundred rooms at realistic concurrency, because hotel voice traffic is sparse and bursty. This gives the privacy story of on-device (audio stays on-premises — a much easier conversation with a hotel's legal team), the per-property vocabulary biasing of cloud, zero per-minute fees, and WAN-outage resilience. What it costs is edge hardware, fleet-wide model management, and accuracy somewhat below the best cloud models.

Recommendation on edge ASR

Do not build it now — it is a distraction from proving the agent. But design the Edge Gateway with enough headroom to host it later (spec the appliance with an upgrade path), and keep AsrProvider implementable at the edge. This becomes your answer when a European or Gulf chain says “guest audio may not leave our premises” — and one of them will.

15.5 The offline command grammar

A fixed, on-device, ~25-phrase recogniser (the same engine family as the wake word handles this well) covering only the actions the device can perform through its local fallback path: lights on/off/dim, temperature up/down, DND on/off, curtains, TV power, and “call the front desk”. When the WAN is down, the device announces the limitation once (“I'm offline right now — I can still do lights, temperature and do-not-disturb”) and operates on this grammar against the Edge Gateway's local rules. Everything else gets a clear refusal and a pointer to the room phone.

Decision D3 — ASR

Build the AsrProvider abstraction first, then bake off Deepgram Nova-3 against Google STT v2 (chirp_2) on your own recorded hotel audio — not on public benchmarks, which are useless here. The evaluation set must be: 500+ real utterances from a live property, weighted toward accented English, with TV and HVAC noise present, including menu items and property proper nouns. Measure WER, but weight entity error rate (did it get the room number, the menu item, the temperature) far higher than raw WER, because that is what determines whether the turn succeeds.

Ship with two vendors wired from day one, selected per property by config. This is not over-engineering: it is how you survive a vendor outage, how you negotiate price, and how you satisfy a chain that insists on a specific processor.

17.1 The phrase cache — the highest-value optimisation in the audio path

A large fraction of what the assistant says is drawn from a small set of phrases: acknowledgements (“Sure, one moment”), confirmations (“Done”, “I've set it to 23 degrees”), clarifications (“Which room would you like that in?”), errors, and greetings. These should be pre-synthesised per property voice per language and cached as Opus on the device itself, not fetched per turn.

  • Latency: the acknowledgement plays in <20 ms instead of ~300 ms — and it is the acknowledgement, not the answer, that determines whether the device feels fast.
  • Cost: plausibly 30–45% of TTS characters eliminated.
  • Resilience: the device can still speak when the TTS vendor is down.

Implementation: a versioned phrase pack per (property_voice, language), pushed to devices on provisioning and on voice-config change, with numeric slot substitution handled by a small set of pre-synthesised number fragments for the most common cases (temperatures 16–30, times, small integers).

18. Cascaded vs speech-to-speech (Gemini Live)

Gemini's Live API accepts audio and emits audio natively, removing two hops. It is genuinely faster and the prosody is better. It is also the wrong primary architecture for this product, for reasons that are about governance rather than capability.

Decision D2 — Pipeline shape

Cascaded is the production path. The intermediate transcript is not an implementation detail — it is the artefact that the audit trail, the redaction pipeline, the eval harness and the safety filters are all built on. Trading it for ~700 ms is a bad trade when §20.4 recovers most of that perceived latency for free.

But build a Live-API lane behind a per-property feature flag, restricted to a defined conversational subset — small talk, local recommendations, property information — where no T2+ action can be taken. Route to it only when the router classifies the turn as chat rather than task. This gives you the best-in-class feel where it is safe, the auditable pipeline where it matters, and real operational data on the Live API before you ever consider promoting it.

20.4 Why perceived latency is the only number that matters

The agentic lane's p95 of 4.3 seconds would be a product-killing figure if the guest experienced it as silence. They do not, because the orchestrator emits a filled pause from the device's cached phrase pack the moment the router selects the agentic lane — before the LLM has even responded. The guest hears “Sure, one moment—” at ~600 ms and then the substantive answer flows in behind it.

This is not a trick; it is how humans handle the same problem. Three rules make it work:

  • Vary the phrase. Rotate through 6–8 acknowledgements per language. A device that says exactly the same three words every time sounds broken within a day.
  • Only fill when you will be slow. Fast-lane turns must never get a filled pause — it makes a 600 ms interaction feel slower, not faster.
  • Never acknowledge an outcome you have not achieved. “One moment” is honest; “Done!” before the BMS has acked is a lie the guest will catch when the lights do not change.

The most under-appreciated risk in this list

Device-to-room mis-binding. If a device is moved during a refurbishment and the catalogue is not updated, a guest in 414 will control the lights in 412 — and the audit log will confidently record it as correct. This is both a guest-safety issue and a privacy issue, and it will not surface as an error anywhere unless you build detection for it. Treat room binding as a continuously verified fact, not a provisioning-time fact.
